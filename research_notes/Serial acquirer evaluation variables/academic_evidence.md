# Academic and large-sample evidence on what makes serial acquirers create or destroy shareholder value

Reading notes on verification level: most academic papers below were verified at abstract / NBER-digest / search-summary level only (full texts were paywalled, timed out, or unreadable PDFs). Where a number comes from a secondary summary rather than the paper itself, it is marked "(secondary)". Anything I could only recall from memory is in the Gaps sections, not in Cited Findings.

## 1. Acquirer returns by deal characteristics (payment method, target type, relative size, frequency, relatedness)

### Takeaway
The most replicated result in the literature is that acquirer announcement returns are negative for public targets (especially when paid in stock) and positive for private and subsidiary targets; small acquirers do better than large ones. For private/subsidiary deals, larger relative size is associated with *higher* returns, so "small deals good, big deals bad" is really "big public, stock-financed deals by large, highly valued acquirers are bad".

### Cited Findings
- Fuller, Netter & Stegemoller (2002, Journal of Finance 57(4)) study firms making five or more acquisitions within a short window (US, 1990s), holding the bidder constant so deal characteristics can be isolated. Bidder returns are significantly negative for public targets paid in stock, insignificant for public targets paid in cash, and significantly positive for private and subsidiary targets regardless of payment method. — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v57y2002i4p1763-1793.html)
- Same paper: for private and subsidiary targets, acquirer abnormal returns rise with the relative size of the target (i.e. the positive effect is larger for bigger private deals). — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v57y2002i4p1763-1793.html)
- Moeller, Schlingemann & Stulz (NBER w9523, published JFE 2004): 12,023 completed US acquisitions of at least $1m by public firms over 1980-2001 (5,583 private targets, 3,798 subsidiary targets, 2,642 public targets), 3-day announcement window. — [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/aug03/big-firms-lose-value-acquisitions)
- Same study: public-firm acquisitions have an average abnormal return of -1.02% and lose 5.9 cents per dollar spent; public targets are the only target type with a negative average return; private and subsidiary acquisitions gain on average. — [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/aug03/big-firms-lose-value-acquisitions)
- Same study (size effect): acquisitions by small firms (bottom 25% of NYSE market cap) have announcement returns 1.55 percentage points higher than comparable acquisitions by large firms; small firms created about $8bn of shareholder wealth, large firms destroyed about $226bn over the 20 years. — [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/aug03/big-firms-lose-value-acquisitions)
- Same study: cash deals outperform equity deals only for public-firm acquisitions. — [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/aug03/big-firms-lose-value-acquisitions)
- Long-run returns by payment method: Loughran & Vijh (1997, Journal of Finance 52(5)), 947 US acquisitions 1970-1989: over five years after the deal, acquirers completing stock mergers earn significantly negative excess returns of -25.0%, while acquirers completing cash tender offers earn significantly positive excess returns. — [RePEc abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:52:y:1997:i:5:p:1765-90)
- Mortal & Schill (JFQA): the poor post-deal returns of stock-financed acquisitions are "more precisely explained" by the larger asset growth that stock deals produce, i.e. the stock-payment penalty is partly the asset-growth effect in disguise. — [JFQA, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/postacquisition-returns-of-stock-deals-evidence-of-the-pervasiveness-of-the-asset-growth-effect/68D4383F00A213F47CFCCA83E0865C59)
- Golubov, Yawson & Zhang (2015, JFE 116(2), "Extraordinary acquirers"): acquirer firm fixed effects alone explain about as much variation in acquirer returns as all firm- and deal-level characteristics combined; the interquartile range of acquirer fixed effects is over 6%. Persistence is strong at the top of the distribution (80th percentile) but not at the mean, and it survives CEO changes. — [RePEc abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:116:y:2015:i:2:p:314-330)
- Renneboog & Vansteenkiste (2019, Journal of Corporate Finance 58, review article): identify serial acquisitions, CEO overconfidence, acquirer-target relatedness/complementarity, and shareholder intervention as key drivers of long-run post-takeover performance, and caution that short-run announcement results often do not carry over to the long run and that heterogeneous measures make unambiguous conclusions hard. — [Tilburg University record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/failure-and-success-in-mergers-and-acquisitions/)
- Timing caveat on cash: a secondary citation of Wang & Xie (2009) reports that cash-financed deals in 2003-2007 did not create value for acquirer shareholders, unlike the 1990s. (secondary, not verified against original) — [EFMA 2011 working paper](https://www.efmaefm.org/0efmameetings/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2011-Braga/papers/0262_update.pdf)

### Inferences
- Variable candidates and their evidence grade:
  - **Share of deals/consideration paid in stock** (or net share issuance as a proxy): negative; strong, replicated in both announcement and 5-year returns. Computable from cash-flow statement (cash paid for acquisitions) and share count.
  - **Private/subsidiary vs public target mix**: private/subsidiary positive; strong and replicated for announcement returns. Computable from deal announcements, not from financial statements. For roll-up style acquirers (Constellation, Topicus) nearly all deals are private, so this variable will have little cross-sectional spread among the index candidates but supports the thesis for the category.
  - **Acquirer size**: smaller is better (about 1.5pp per deal); strong. Trivially computable, but arguably not a quality signal an index should reward.
  - **Relative deal size**: direction depends on target type (positive for private, negative for large public stock deals). A simple "largest deal / market cap" or "deal spend concentration" metric is defensible mainly as a tail-risk screen (see section 2 and 3), not as a monotonic score.
- The Golubov et al. result is the best academic support for the premise of a serial-acquirer index: acquirer skill is a persistent firm trait, concentrated in a top group, and not well captured by observable deal characteristics. It also implies a track-record variable (historical ROIC on acquired capital, or past post-deal performance) may be more informative than deal-characteristic variables.

### Gaps
- Exact CARs from Fuller-Netter-Stegemoller (sample of 539 acquirers / 3,135 bids and specific CARs by cell are commonly quoted, but I could not open the paper to confirm; the UGA PDF returned 403). One secondary summary mentioned roughly +1.9% for private targets; unverified.
- Related vs diversifying deals: I did not retrieve a primary large-sample source with effect sizes (e.g. Morck-Shleifer-Vishny 1990 on diversifying deals, or the conglomerate-discount literature). The only cited support is the Renneboog-Vansteenkiste review naming relatedness as a long-run driver. Treat direction (related better) as plausible but not quantified here. Relatedness is also hard to compute from financial statements; segment/SIC overlap from deal announcements would be needed.
- Loughran-Vijh cash tender-offer magnitude: a "+61.7%" figure appears in secondary sources and one secondary table shows 18.5%; I could not reconcile these, so only the sign is reported.
- Masulis-Wang-Xie (2007) on governance and Maloney-McCormick-Mitchell (1993) on leverage were searched but not retrieved.

## 2. Declining returns across a serial acquirer's deal sequence (hubris, learning, empire building, large-loss deals)

### Takeaway
On average, announcement returns decline with deal order, but the interpretation is contested (hubris vs rational learning vs opportunity-set depletion vs market anticipation), and the newest evidence finds the decline is absent for the most frequent acquirers once anticipation is controlled for. The robust practical lesson is about the tail: a tiny number of very large deals by highly valued acquirers account for nearly all aggregate value destruction.

### Cited Findings
- Billett & Qian (2008, Management Science 54(6)): looking at individual CEOs' acquisition histories (US public targets), first deals show no significant announcement effect, later deals show negative announcement returns; prior positive deal experience does not curb the negative effect, and CEOs increase net purchases of own stock before later deals. Interpreted as self-attribution bias leading to overconfidence. — [RePEc abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:54:y:2008:i:6:p:1037-1051)
- Doukas & Petmezas (working paper, UK private-target deals): higher-order acquisitions (five or more deals within three years) are associated with lower wealth effects than first deals. — [EFMA 2006 paper](https://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2006-Madrid/papers/857394_full.pdf)
- Aktas, de Bodt & Roll (2009, Journal of Corporate Finance 15(5)): theoretical result that a declining CAR from deal to deal is not sufficient evidence of hubris; rational, learning CEOs who bid more aggressively as they learn would produce the same pattern. They derive predictions on time between deals to separate the stories. Their earlier working-paper version confirms the declining trend exists on average. — [RePEc abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:corfin:v:15:y:2009:i:5:p:543-561)
- Aktas, de Bodt & Roll (2011, Journal of Corporate Finance 17(1)): on successive acquisitions of large US public targets, CEOs (both rational and hubristic) adjust bidding to investor reactions to prior deals, consistent with learning. — [RePEc abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:corfin:v:17:y:2011:i:1:p:18-32)
- Morillon (2021, Financial Review 56(3)): classifying acquirers by acquisition pattern, the decline in returns is concentrated in bursts of acquisitions; there is no evidence of decline for the most active acquirers; evidence is consistent with temporary overvaluation, agency costs and bidder learning. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/finrev/v56y2021i3p407-432.html)
- Macias, Rau & Stouraitis (2025, Review of Corporate Finance Studies 14(1), "Solving Serial Acquirer Puzzles"): controlling for market anticipation, the most frequent serial acquirers do not earn declining returns as they continue acquiring, while less frequent acquirers do. — [RCFS, OUP](https://academic.oup.com/rcfs/article/14/1/35/7225178)
- Moeller, Schlingemann & Stulz (2005, Journal of Finance 60(2), "Wealth Destruction on a Massive Scale?"): in 1998-2001 US acquirers lost about $240bn at announcement. "Large loss deals" (announcement losses of $1bn or more) were 2.1% of acquisitions but 43.4% of dollars spent; they lost $397bn in aggregate while all other deals gained $157bn; their average announcement abnormal return was -10.6%. — [NBER w10200](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10200)
- Same study: firms making large-loss deals had high Tobin's q and low book-to-market relative to other acquirers, and typically had been successful acquirers before; the authors' reading is that highly valued firms made poor acquisitions once good opportunities ran out. — [NBER w10200](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10200)
- Same authors, 20-year sample: over 87% of acquisition dollars were spent after 1993 and losses are concentrated in 1994-2001; excluding those years the aggregate loss on public-firm acquisitions shrinks from $257bn to $10.4bn. — [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/aug03/big-firms-lose-value-acquisitions)

### Inferences
- "Number of deals done" is not itself a negative signal. The declining-returns result holds for occasional and burst acquirers, not for steady, high-frequency programmes. This squares the academic literature with the consulting "programmatic M&A" findings in section 3.
- Variables suggested by this evidence:
  - **Steadiness of deal cadence** (e.g. low coefficient of variation of annual acquisition spend, or share of years with at least one deal) rather than raw deal count: steady is good, bursty is bad. Evidence: moderate (two recent papers, announcement-return based). Computable from cash-flow statements (acquisition spend per year) and deal lists.
  - **Step-change in deal size relative to own history** (largest deal / market cap, or latest-year acquisition spend vs 5-year average): a jump is the classic large-loss-deal setup. Evidence: strong for the tail event, though from one merger wave (US 1998-2001). Computable.
  - **Valuation at time of dealmaking** (high q / low book-to-market): negative. Evidence: strong for large-loss deals. Computable, but penalising high multiples would mechanically penalise the best-regarded compounders, so it is a design choice rather than a clean quality signal.
- Announcement CARs are a weak yardstick for serial acquirers of small private firms: individual deals are too small to move the stock and are anticipated by the market (the Macias et al. point). Long-run and accounting-based measures (section 5) are better suited to scoring.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes for the deal-order decline (e.g. first-deal vs fifth-deal CAR in Fuller-Netter-Stegemoller, commonly quoted as roughly 1.7% falling to about 0.5%) could not be verified from the primary text.
- Sample sizes/periods for Morillon (2021) and Macias et al. (2025) were not retrieved.
- No direct evidence found on empire-building proxies (e.g. CEO pay tied to size) that would be computable from financial statements.

## 3. Consulting-firm large-sample studies: McKinsey programmatic M&A, Bain, BCG

### Takeaway
All three firms independently find that frequent acquirers doing many small/mid deals outperform occasional or large-deal acquirers on long-run TSR by roughly 2-5 percentage points a year, with lower dispersion. These are descriptive sorts, not peer-reviewed, with obvious survivorship and reverse-causality issues (successful companies can afford to keep buying).

### Cited Findings
- McKinsey (Global 2,000 dataset, ten-year windows): programmatic acquirers had a median excess TSR of 2.1% a year; the other M&A approaches were zero or negative. — [McKinsey, "The seven habits of programmatic acquirers"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/m-and-a/our-insights/the-seven-habits-of-programmatic-acquirers)
- McKinsey Quarterly: programmatic acquirers delivered about 2 percentage points more excess TSR per year than peers; none of the other approaches (organic, selective, large deal) created excess TSR on average. Programmatic was also the least risky: smallest deviation in performance and the largest share of companies with positive excess TSR (65%). — [McKinsey, "How one approach to M&A is more likely to create value than all others"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/how-one-approach-to-m-and-a-is-more-likely-to-create-value-than-all-others)
- McKinsey: programmatic acquirers were 12% of companies in the dataset but accounted for 28% of deal value on average between 2007 and 2019. — [McKinsey, "The seven habits of programmatic acquirers"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/m-and-a/our-insights/the-seven-habits-of-programmatic-acquirers)
- McKinsey definition as reported by CFO.com: programmatic M&A is several small or mid-sized deals that together reach at least 15% of market capitalisation; the latest update puts the programmatic premium at 3.9% excess TSR over the past decade versus 2.9% in the 2010s; the companies doing the most deals earned the highest returns; during COVID-19 programmatic was the only strategy with positive excess returns (selective flat, organic and large-deal negative). (secondary) — [CFO.com](https://www.cfo.com/news/whats-the-best-ma-strategy-think-small-and-steady/701467/)
- McKinsey: companies that combine a large deal with a programmatic approach earn about 1 percentage point more TSR a year than peers and are more successful on their large deals. (as summarised in search results from McKinsey's large-deals article; not opened) — [McKinsey, "The flip side of large M&A deals"](https://www.mckinsey.com.br/capabilities/m-and-a/our-insights/the-flip-side-of-large-m-and-a-deals)
- Bain (2022 M&A Report): 2,897 public companies worldwide, 10-year annual TSR. Companies with no deals (16% of the sample): 5.4%. Frequent acquirers, about one deal a year ("serial bolt-ons"): 10.1%. "Mountain climbers" (frequent and material, i.e. cumulative acquired value large relative to market cap; 334 companies): 11.0%. "Selective large bets" (infrequent, large relative to market cap; 2010-2020): 5.1%, the worst group. — [Bain M&A Report 2022](https://bain.com/insights/how-to-create-value-m-and-a-report-2022)
- BCG (2015 M&A report, about 19,000 companies, 1990-2014): "portfolio builders" (more than five deals in five years) averaged 11.6% annual TSR and "active buyers" 8.8%, versus 3.5% for one-timers and non-acquirers. One-year relative TSR after announcement: one-timers 2% (only 43% of such deals positive), active buyers 6%, portfolio builders 8%. (press-release summary) — [BCG via GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2015/10/12/924250/0/en/Strategic-Acquirers-Create-More-Value-Than-Nonacquisitive-Companies.html?print=1)
- BCG (2016, deals 1991-2015): "portfolio masters" (at least five major transactions, buying or selling, in five years) generated average relative TSR of 4.1%, more than 1pp above one-timers, even though one-timers received the largest short-term announcement reaction (average CAR +5.5%). — [BCG, "How Portfolio Masters Make M&A Work for Shareholders"](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2016/how-portfolio-masters-make-m-a-work-shareholders)

### Inferences
- Variable candidates:
  - **Deal frequency** (deals per year, or years-with-a-deal over the last 5-10): positive; consistent across three independent large samples (about 2,000 to 19,000 firms, global, 1990-2020s) but non-peer-reviewed and descriptive. Computable from deal announcements.
  - **Average deal size relative to market cap** (small is good) and **cumulative programme materiality** (McKinsey: at least 15% of market cap in aggregate; Bain: high cumulative share is best when combined with frequency): the winning profile is "many small deals that add up to something material". A two-part variable (frequency high, single-deal concentration low) captures this.
  - **Largest single deal as % of market cap**: negative when not embedded in a frequent programme (Bain's selective large bets at 5.1% vs 10-11%).
- Consulting and academic evidence agree on the high-frequency end (section 2: no declining returns for the most frequent acquirers) and on the danger of infrequent large deals. They differ in yardstick (long-run TSR vs announcement CAR), which is why BCG can find one-timers get the best announcement pop but the worst subsequent returns.
- Caution for the report writer: TSR sorts are not risk-adjusted beyond industry/peer benchmarking, classification is done over the same window as returns are measured (look-ahead), and firms that stumble stop acquiring and drop out of the "frequent" bucket. These studies justify the theme more than they validate a predictive scoring variable.

### Gaps
- McKinsey's exact category thresholds (e.g. programmatic commonly described as more than two small/mid deals a year; large deal as a single deal above 30% of market cap) and per-category medians could not be read directly; both McKinsey pages timed out. The 2.1% / ~2% / 2.3% / 3.9% figures come from different editions and windows, so cite the specific edition.
- A 2026 Bain statement that frequent acquirers' TSR advantage over inactive companies widened from 57% (2000-2010) to 130% (2012-2022) surfaced in search but I could not tie it to a primary URL.
- No consulting study found that tests these classifications out of sample (classify first, measure returns afterwards).

## 4. Leverage, free cash flow / agency, goodwill impairments and equity issuance

### Takeaway
Evidence supports Jensen's free-cash-flow concern: acquisitions by firms sitting on excess cash destroy value, and overvalued equity used as acquisition currency predicts later goodwill write-offs and poor returns. Goodwill impairments are a lagging-but-informative marker of failed deals; equity-financed asset growth is one of the more robust negative return predictors.

### Cited Findings
- Harford (1999, Journal of Finance 54(6), US): cash-rich firms are more likely to attempt acquisitions; their acquisitions are value-decreasing, destroying about seven cents of value per excess dollar of cash reserves held; mergers by cash-rich bidders are followed by abnormal declines in operating performance. Interpreted as support for the agency costs of free cash flow. — [RePEc abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:54:y:1999:i:6:p:1969-1997)
- Gu & Lev (2011, The Accounting Review; working-paper title "Investor Sentiments, Ill-Advised Acquisitions and Goodwill Impairment"): share overpricing at the time of acquisition predicts goodwill write-offs and their magnitude; acquisitions by overpriced companies are often ill-advised and worsen post-acquisition negative returns beyond the mere reversal of overpricing. — [NYU archive](https://archive.nyu.edu/handle/2451/27476)
- University at Buffalo summary of the same study: the most overpriced companies that acquired with stock wrote off up to 13 times more goodwill than the least overpriced. (secondary) — [UB School of Management](https://management.buffalo.edu/about/news.host.html/content/shared/mgt/news/guresearch.detail.html)
- Li, Shroff, Venkataraman & Zhang (Review of Accounting Studies): goodwill impairment is a leading indicator of a decline in future profitability; investors and analysts revise expectations downward on impairment announcements. — [Springer, RAST](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11142-011-9167-2)
- Moeller-Schlingemann-Stulz large-loss deals were made by firms with high q / low book-to-market, i.e. richly valued equity (see section 2). — [NBER w10200](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10200)
- Stock-financed acquirers: -25.0% five-year excess return (Loughran & Vijh, section 1). — [RePEc abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:52:y:1997:i:5:p:1765-90)
- Fu ("Dissecting the Asset Growth Anomaly"): high-asset-growth firms expand by issuing large amounts of debt and equity or through externally financed acquisitions; the paper argues the asset-growth return effect is largely the external-financing anomaly plus a delisting bias. — [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/2015/03/daily-academic-alpha-sham-strategy-or-accounting-alpha/)

### Inferences
- Variable candidates:
  - **Net share issuance / dilution** (change in diluted shares over 3-5 years): negative; strong and replicated across payment-method, goodwill and external-financing literatures. Computable from financial statements. This is probably the single cleanest "how are the deals funded" variable for the tool.
  - **Self-funding ratio** (acquisition spend covered by operating/free cash flow rather than new equity or debt): positive by implication of the external-financing evidence. Computable from the cash-flow statement.
  - **Goodwill impairments** (cumulative impairments / cumulative goodwill acquired, or any impairment in the last N years): negative; moderate evidence as a marker of past overpayment and a leading indicator of weaker profitability. Computable. Note IFRS/US GAAP impairment timing is discretionary and lagging, so absence of impairment is weak positive evidence.
  - **Excess cash balance**: negative per Harford, but the sample is US 1977-1993 and the mechanism (idle cash burning a hole) is the opposite of the disciplined-reinvestment model of the best serial acquirers. Low priority as a scoring variable.
- Leverage: the theoretical prior (Jensen: debt disciplines managers) and the practical risk (levered roll-ups failing when credit tightens) point in opposite directions, and I found no retrieved large-sample evidence to settle it. Suggest treating leverage (net debt / EBITDA) as a risk guardrail with a threshold rather than a monotonic score, and labelling it as judgment-based rather than evidence-based.

### Gaps
- Leverage and acquirer returns: Maloney, McCormick & Mitchell (1993), usually cited for higher-leverage acquirers earning better announcement returns, was not retrieved. No effect size available.
- Li & Sloan ("Has goodwill accounting gone bad?", on impairments being untimely and predictable, with goodwill-heavy/low-profitability firms underperforming) was not retrieved; only cited indirectly.
- Net share issuance as a general return predictor (Pontiff & Woodgate 2008; Daniel & Titman 2006; Fama & French 2008 "Dissecting Anomalies", which I recall finding net stock issues and accruals among the most pervasive anomalies while asset growth was weaker among large caps): search returned no usable source. From memory only; verify before citing.
- Jensen (1986) itself is theory; the empirical citation here is Harford only. Lang-Stulz-Walkling (1991) on low-q, high-cash-flow bidders was not retrieved.

## 5. Accounting metrics that predict long-run stock returns for acquisitive firms (ROIC, asset growth, issuance, accruals, organic growth)

### Takeaway
Total asset growth is a strong negative predictor of subsequent returns, and acquisition-driven growth is part of it, which is a headwind every serial acquirer must overcome with high returns on the capital deployed. The evidence favours scoring (a) how growth is financed, (b) organic vs acquired growth, and (c) returns on invested capital, though direct large-sample evidence on ROIC specifically for serial acquirers was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Cooper, Gulen & Schill (2008, Journal of Finance 63(4), US stocks 1968-2003): year-on-year percentage change in total assets strongly and negatively predicts subsequent abnormal returns; the effect starts the following year, persists up to five years, and holds among large-cap stocks. — [RePEc abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:63:y:2008:i:4:p:1609-1651)
- Magnitude: low-asset-growth stocks earned about 20% a year more than high-asset-growth stocks over roughly 40 years (about 26% vs 4% average annual return for extreme deciles, equal-weighted). (secondary) — [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/the-asset-growth-effect-in-stock-returns/)
- International replication: higher asset growth is followed by lower returns across 40 international equity markets. (secondary) — [Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/asset-growth-effect)
- Decomposition: a conference paper splitting asset growth into internal expansion and acquisitions notes the Cooper et al. measure is tilted toward acquiring firms and reports a mean first-year abnormal return of -8.77% for firms whose high growth is due to acquisitions. — [EFMA 2008 paper (Chan et al.)](https://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2008-Athens/papers/Chan.pdf)
- Mortal & Schill (JFQA): post-acquisition underperformance of stock deals is explained by the asset-growth effect; once asset growth is controlled for, the stock-deal penalty largely disappears. — [JFQA, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/postacquisition-returns-of-stock-deals-evidence-of-the-pervasiveness-of-the-asset-growth-effect/68D4383F00A213F47CFCCA83E0865C59)
- Contested: Fu argues the asset-growth anomaly is almost completely driven by delisting bias in CRSP and the external-financing anomaly, with no independent asset-growth effect after controls. — [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/2015/03/daily-academic-alpha-sham-strategy-or-accounting-alpha/); contradicted by Lipson, Mortal & Schill, who find the effect pervasive and attribute contrary evidence to specification choices — [JFQA, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/on-the-scope-and-drivers-of-the-asset-growth-effect/B8CFCF68A82E273E979221EF26A77096)
- Related investment evidence: Titman, Wei & Xie (2004) show firms that invest more have lower subsequent stock returns. (secondary citation) — [NBER w21533, Stambaugh & Yuan](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21533/w21533.pdf)
- Profitability: Novy-Marx (2013, JFE 108(1)) documents a gross-profitability premium; later work finds higher profitability is associated with abnormally high returns among profitable firms. (secondary citation) — [IDEAS, "Mispricing Factors"](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/21533.html)
- Post-deal operating performance deteriorates for cash-rich bidders (Harford, section 4), and goodwill impairment leads declines in profitability (section 4). — [RePEc abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:54:y:1999:i:6:p:1969-1997)

### Inferences
Suggested shortlist for the 5-7 scoring variables, with direction, evidence strength and computability:

| # | Variable | Direction | Evidence strength | Computable from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Net share issuance / dilution over 3-5 years (proxy for stock-financed deals) | Lower is better | Strong, replicated (payment method, external financing, goodwill literature) | Financial statements |
| 2 | Deal cadence: frequency and steadiness of acquisition activity | Higher/steadier is better | Moderate; three large consulting samples plus recent academic work on frequent acquirers; descriptive | Deal announcements; cash-flow statement |
| 3 | Deal-size concentration: largest deal (or peak-year spend) as % of market cap or of own history | Lower is better | Strong for the tail (large-loss deals); consulting sorts agree | Deal announcements; cash-flow statement |
| 4 | ROIC (ideally including goodwill and acquired intangibles), level and trend as the programme scales | Higher/stable is better | Indirect: profitability premium plus the need to offset the asset-growth drag; no serial-acquirer-specific large-sample test found | Financial statements |
| 5 | Self-funded growth: acquisition spend covered by free cash flow; asset growth financed internally rather than externally | Higher is better | Moderate to strong (asset-growth and external-financing anomalies; contested which one dominates) | Financial statements |
| 6 | Goodwill impairment history (cumulative impairments / goodwill acquired) | Lower is better | Moderate; lagging indicator | Financial statements |
| 7 | Organic growth (revenue growth excluding acquisitions) | Higher is better | Weak/indirect: decomposition evidence that acquired growth carries the return penalty; depends on company disclosure | Company disclosure (not standardised) |

- Raw asset growth should not be used as a positive score: taken literally the academic evidence says penalise it. For a serial-acquirer universe where all candidates grow assets quickly, the discriminating question is growth quality (variables 1, 4, 5), not growth quantity.
- Private-target share (section 1) is strongly supported but likely near-constant across the candidate set; acquirer size is supported but is not a quality attribute.
- Leverage is best treated as a guardrail (section 4), not an evidence-backed monotonic score.

### Gaps
- ROIC as a predictor specifically for serial acquirers: no peer-reviewed large-sample study found. Practitioner literature (e.g. on Constellation-style compounders) asserts it, but that is outside this evidence base.
- Accruals (Sloan 1996) and their relevance to acquirers (acquisition accounting distorting accruals) were not researched within the call budget; no source retrieved.
- Organic growth vs returns for acquirers: only the EFMA decomposition paper speaks to it; no clean effect size for "high organic growth acquirers outperform".
- Exact Cooper-Gulen-Schill decile returns were taken from a secondary blog summary, not the paper's tables; verify before quoting the 20% figure. The effect is much smaller on a value-weighted, large-cap basis (my recollection; not sourced here).
- Geography: nearly all academic evidence is US (CRSP/SDC), with the UK (Doukas-Petmezas) and a 40-market asset-growth replication as exceptions. No Canada-specific large-sample evidence was found, which matters given the candidate list (Constellation, Topicus, WSP, TFI, Aecon).
